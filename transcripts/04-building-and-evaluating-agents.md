# Building and evaluating AI Agents - Sayash Kapoor, AI Snake Oil

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d5EltXhbcfA

The topic of our conference today is agents at work. Unfortunately, for the next 18 minutes you'll have to listen to me talk about why agents are n't working very well today and how we can improve in AI development. There is currently a huge interest in agents from all sides: in the world of products, in industry, in academic laboratories and research. If you think companies won't be able to scale language models to the level of AGI, then in the near future we will increasingly see agents that are not deployed directly, but function as small parts of larger products and systems. And this is what AI will likely look like in the near future.

Swix proposed several dozen definitions of AI agents. Here is one of them: language models control the operation of a particular system. In fact, even when people naively perceive ChatGPT and Claude as models, these tools are examples of rudimentary agents at some level. They have filters at the inlet and outlet. They can perform certain tasks. They can call these tools and so on. So in a sense, agents are already widely used and successful. We are already seeing mass-produced products that are capable of much more. OpenAI's Operator can perform complex tasks on the Internet. The Deep Research tool can perform 30- minute report creation tasks on any topic.

This is the first reason why I believe today's conference is timely. The second reason is that, on the other hand, ambitious visions of what agents are capable of are still far from being realized. On the left here is a vision of what agents can do. Something like science fiction movies like "Her," and on the right, how these ambitious products have so far failed in the real world. I point this out not to criticize the specific products on the slide, but to sincerely challenge the audience: to create AI agents that actually work for the people who will use them. So throughout this talk, I will talk about three main reasons why agents are still not working, and what we can do to realize their potential and overcome these failures.

The first reason is that it is really difficult to evaluate agents. To start, let's look at a few examples of how agents have failed in the real world when trying to implement them in production. DoNotPay is an American startup that claimed to automate all the work of a lawyer. The startup's co-founder even offered a million dollars to any lawyer who would agree to speak before the U.S. Supreme Court using DoNotPay in their headset. In fact, a few years later, or more recently, the FTC fined DoNotPay hundreds of thousands of dollars. The reason for the fine was that the claims about effectiveness made by DoNotPay turned out to be completely false.

You might think this is a case of a hastily created startup making claims that are not backed by facts. So let's look at the work of more well-known and reputable companies. Law firms LexisNexis and Westlaw are considered among the leaders in legal technology in the United States. A few years ago, LexisNexis released a product that they claimed prevented hallucinations when creating legal reports and justifications. But when Stanford researchers evaluated the LexisNexis and Westlaw products, they found that in a third of cases, or at least a sixth, these language models were hallucinating. In particular, in some cases, these hallucinations completely distorted the content of the original legal text.

In others, the paragraphs were simply made up. They have about 200 examples of such errors in leading products for lawyers. We have also heard claims that AI agents will soon automate all scientific research. Here is an example from the startup Sakana.ai. Sakana claimed to have created a scientific researcher capable of fully automating open scientific developments. Our team at Princeton wanted to test this claim in real-world settings, as automating scientific research is one of our main interests. So we created a benchmark test. We created this test called CoreBench. The tasks in this test are much simpler than you might expect from real scientific research. They only try to reproduce the results of the article, even providing the agent with the necessary code and data to reproduce it.

As you can see, it's much easier than automating all of science. We found that the best agents today cannot even reliably automate scientific research. Less than 40% of article results can be reproduced by leading agents. Of course, one can see that these models are improving, and even if an agent can provide 40% reproducibility , this is a huge step forward, as researchers spend a lot of time reproducing the basic results. But on this basis, it is too hasty to claim that AI will soon automate all of science or that agents will make scientific researchers obsolete. In fact, when people analyzed the work of the Sakana AI scientist, it turned out that it was only tested on toy tasks, where another language model, not human experts, provided the assessment.

And in fact , if you look closely at the results, they turned out to be just minor additions to other scientific works. This is more the level of student research projects, rather than the complete automation of all science. A few days ago, while preparing slides for this talk, I came across another announcement by Sakana AI about creating an agent for optimizing CUDA cores. The declared results were truly impressive. They were supposedly able to provide a 150x speedup compared to standard CUDA cores in PyTorch. The problem is that if you delve deeper into their statements, you will find that they claimed to exceed the theoretical maximum of H100 by 30 times.

This claim was obviously false, and again due to a lack of thorough verification. It turned out that the agent was simply manipulating the reward function instead of actually optimizing the CUDA cores. The point is not to criticize one company, but to emphasize : evaluating agents is a truly complex problem. It needs to be considered a top priority in AI development, otherwise we risk continuing to encounter failures like the one on this slide. The second reason why building agents for the real world is difficult is that static benchmarks can be misleading about their true performance. This is because for a long time we focused on creating tests that are well suited to assessing common language models.

But agents are not the same as language models. For example, to evaluate most language models, it is sufficient to consider the input and output text. These are the domains in which language models operate. This is really enough to create an estimate. On the other hand, agents must perform actions in the real world . They need to interact with the environment. Therefore, building an assessment system that creates virtual environments for these agents to work in is a much more complex problem. Another difficulty is that for LLM, the cost of estimation is limited by the length of the model's context window. We can say that these estimates have a fixed "ceiling".

But when it comes to agents capable of limitless action in the real world, there is no such limit. You can imagine how these agents call other subordinate agents. There may be recursion. There could be all sorts of systems out there, maybe just LLM calls in a for loop. And because of this, cost should once again become the primary metric in all agent evaluations. If you don't have cost as a criterion alongside accuracy or performance, you won't understand how well your agent is performing. Finally, when you create a new benchmark for a language model, you can assume that you are able to evaluate every language model against that benchmark.

But when it comes to evaluating agents, they are often highly specialized . So, for example, if there is a programming agent that you want to evaluate, you cannot use a web benchmark to evaluate it. This leads to the second obstacle: how to create meaningful multidimensional metrics to evaluate agents, instead of relying on a single benchmark. All of these concerns may seem theoretical . You might rightly ask why we care if static evaluations aren't exactly effective for agents. The reason is that due to differences in cost and accuracy, as well as excessive attention to a single benchmark, we cannot get a holistic picture of an agent's performance.

So at Princeton, we developed an agent leaderboard that tries to solve some of these problems. Specifically, for example, for the Core Bench leaderboard I mentioned earlier, you can see several agents ranked by cost and accuracy. So, on this Pareto frontier, you can see that agents like Claude 3.5 score about the same as OpenAI’s o1 model, but Claude costs $57, while o1 costs $664. Even if OpenAI’s o1 performance were a few percent higher, which it wasn’t, the choice is clear to most AI engineers. You can choose a model that costs 10 times less and works about the same any day. In response to this two-dimensional Pareto chart, I have often been asked: Are LLMs becoming too cheap to measure?

In other words, why should we even worry about the cost of launching an agent if the costs of building these models are falling rapidly? And it is indeed true that costs have come down significantly over the past few years. If you compare DaVinci 003 , OpenAI's 2022 model, to today's GPT-4o mini, which outperforms the older model in most cases, the cost has decreased by more than a hundred times. But at the same time, if you are building applications that require scaling, this approach is still quite expensive. Especially from a prototyping perspective : one of the barriers for AI engineers is the need to iterate openly.

So if you don't factor in expenses, your prototype can quickly start costing you thousands of dollars. Finally, even if the cost of executing LLM calls during inference continues to fall, the so-called Jevons paradox, I suspect, will lead to an increase in the overall cost of agent operations. Jevons' paradox is a theory by a 19th- century British economist who found that as the cost of coal production decreased, overall coal consumption in many industries did not decrease, but increased. The same thing happened when ATMs began to be installed throughout the United States. People expected bank tellers to lose their jobs. But the exact opposite happened. Because ATMs were so easy to install, the number of bank branches increased dramatically, leading to an increase in the number of tellers hired.

I expect something similar to happen now, as the cost of language models continues to plummet. This is why, at least for the foreseeable future, we must consider cost when it comes to evaluating agents. So how do we automate this process? With the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL), we have found a way to automatically score agents on these 11 different tests, and there will be many more soon. Furthermore, even if we create these multidimensional benchmarks and value-controlled valuations, there are still some problems with this type of testing, as agent benchmarks have become the metric by which venture capitalists fund companies. An example is Cosine, which raised a seed round of investment based on its results in the SWE-bench.

In fact, agent developer Cognition raised $175 million at a $2 billion valuation, largely due to their agent performing excellently on the SWE-bench. Unfortunately, benchmark results very rarely reflect real- world performance. So, this is a great analysis of how well Devon is doing. Devon is an agent developed by Cognition with the support of the incredibly talented people at answer.ai. Well, instead of relying on standard tests, they tried to implement Devon in real-world conditions. And it turned out that in a month of use, they tested it on 20 different tasks, and only three of them were successful. So this is another reason why over-reliance on static tests can be very misleading.

How do we overcome this? One of my favorite ways to think about this is a work by Berkeley researchers called " Who Tests the Testers." Above is a typical evaluation process consisting of single queries to LLM based on static metrics, which is, in fact, the flawed AI evaluation paradigm we just discussed. And below is what they offer. They propose to involve expert people who proactively edit the criteria on which these LLM assessments are based, ultimately leading to significantly better results. This brings me to the final key takeaway about why agent performance doesn’t always match real-world conditions—it’s a confusion between capabilities and reliability. Roughly speaking, capabilities mean what a model can do at certain points in time.

For those who are technically savvy, this means “pass at K” accuracy for large K. This means that one of the K answers the model produces is correct. On the other hand, reliability means consistently performing a task correctly every time. When agents are deployed to make important decisions in the real world, the focus needs to be on reliability, not capabilities. This is because language models are already capable of so many things. But if you convince yourself that this means a solid experience for the end user, that's when real products fail. In particular, I believe that model training methods that give us 90% of the results—what in Swix's terminology is the work of an ML engineer— do not necessarily provide 99.999%, known as the "five nines" of reliability.

And bridging this gap between 90% and 99.9% is the job of an AI engineer. And I think that's what led to the failure of products like the Humane Pin and Rabbit R1. This happened because the developers did not foresee that the lack of reliability in such products would lead to their failure. In other words, if your personal assistant only orders food from DoorDash correctly 80% of the time, from a product perspective, it’s a catastrophic failure. So, one of the proposed ways to solve this problem to increase reliability is to create a verifier, something like a unit test. And on that basis, there have been a lot of claims that if we can improve the scalability of the inferences of these tools, we will get very robust models.

Unfortunately, we found that verifiers can also be imperfect in practice. For example, two leading coding benchmarks, HumanEval, contain false positives in their unit tests. That is, the model may produce incorrect code, but still pass the unit test. And once we take these false positives into account, we see that the inference scaling curves start to curve downward. So instead of the model's performance continuing to increase if your verifiers have false positives, the model's performance drops because the harder you try, the more likely you are to get the wrong answer. So this is also not a perfect solution to the reliability problem. So what is the solution then?

I believe the challenge for AI engineers is to figure out what software optimizations and abstractions are needed to work with stochastic components like LLM . In other words, this is a system design problem, not just a marginal improvement problem , where you need to work with the constraints of a stochastic system . And I want to emphasize at the last minute of my talk that this means looking at AI engineering more as a field of reliability than as a field of software development or machine learning. This also brings me to a clear mindset shift that is necessary to become a successful AI engineer. If you look at the title slide of my talk, it points to one of the areas where we have already overcome some of the limitations of stochastic systems —the emergence of computing.

The 1946 ENIAC computer used over 17,000 vacuum tubes, many of which failed so frequently in its early days that the computer was unavailable half the time. And the engineers who created this product understood that from the end user's perspective, it was a failure. So their main task in the first two years of working with this computer was to eliminate reliability issues to bring it to a state where it would be functional enough for the end user. And I argue that this is what AI engineers should think of as their main job. The point is not to create perfect products, although that is important, but to eliminate the reliability problems that plague every agent that uses stochastic models as a foundation.

So, that's what I leave you with today. To become successful engineers , you need to change your approach and start thinking of yourself as the people who ensure that the next wave of computing is as reliable as possible for end users. And there are many precedents that similar things have happened in the past. Okay, finally, I'll leave you with three main conclusions. It was nice to be here. Thank you.
